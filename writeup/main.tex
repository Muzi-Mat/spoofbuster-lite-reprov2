\title{Reproducing Minutiae Clustering in Fingerprint Spoof Buster Lite, Without the Paper's Data}
\author{Bheki Sithole (3035571), University of the Witwatersrand\\
Repository: \url{https://github.com/muzi-mat/spoofbuster-lite-reprov2}}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Chugh and Jain~\cite{chugh2019} report that classifying patches at 10 k-means centroids of the minutiae,
instead of at every minutia, keeps fingerprint presentation-attack detection at TDR~=~95.3\% (against
95.7\%) at FDR~=~0.2\%, while cutting phone inference time from 1,554~ms to 302~ms. The paper's data is
gated by a Michigan State University agreement and could not be obtained, so the full pipeline was
reimplemented to test the claim's mechanism on seeded synthetic fingerprints. With a detector that reaches \TDRall\% with all minutiae,
10 centroid patches cost \DeltaSeedsMean~$\pm$~\DeltaSeedsSD\ points of TDR across \NSeeds\ training seeds
(range \DeltaSeedsMin\ to \DeltaSeedsMax), and none of the seeds met the one-point criterion; at
FDR~=~1\% the loss is a consistent \DeltaOneSeedsMean~$\pm$~\DeltaOneSeedsSD. Centring each patch on the
minutia nearest its centroid recovered most of the loss. Clustering cut CNN time \SpeedupCNN$\times$ but
total latency only \SpeedupTotal$\times$, because minutiae extraction is a fixed cost. The paper's
Table~2 is also shown to be internally inconsistent.
\end{abstract}

\section{The claim}
Fingerprint Spoof Buster~\cite{chugh2018} detects presentation attacks (spoofs) by cutting a
$96\times96$ patch around every minutia, aligned to the minutia direction, scoring each patch with
MobileNet-v1~\cite{howard2017} and averaging the scores. Its cost grows with the number of minutiae, 35
per image in the paper's data. Section~3 of~\cite{chugh2019} proposes Spoof Buster Lite: cluster the
minutia coordinates with k-means, cut one patch at each centroid, and average the patch scores weighted
by cluster size.

The claim reproduced here is in Table~2 of~\cite{chugh2019}: \emph{with $k=10$ clusters, TDR at
FDR~=~0.2\% falls only from 95.7\% to 95.3\%, while inference time falls from 1,554~ms to 302~ms.} TDR is
the fraction of spoofs detected, and FDR the fraction of bona fide prints wrongly flagged. The support
is that one table: six values of $k$ and the baseline, each given as mean $\pm$ s.d.\ over ``5-fold
cross-validation'', with times from a quantised model on a Redmi Note~4. The data are 5,743 bona fide
and 4,912 spoof images of 12 materials (MSU-FPAD and PBSKD).

My research question, fixed in the proposal: \emph{does $k=10$ keep TDR within one percentage point of
the all-minutiae baseline while reducing the number of MobileNet evaluations?}

\section{Route and setup}
\textbf{Data access.} I asked the first author, Tarang Chugh, for access. He replied on 26 October 2026
that he had access only as a PhD student, no longer has it and may not share it, and referred me to the
MSU lab. I wrote to the lab and had no response by the deadline. Other legitimate sources were
searched. The closest substitute, LivDet~2015~\cite{livdet}, includes a CrossMatch subset but
also requires a signed licence. I did not use unofficial copies.

\textbf{Synthetic stand-in.} So that the method could still be tested end to end, a seeded
dataset was generated: 1,600 bona fide prints (\NTestBona\ for testing) and 6 artefact ``materials'' of 200 prints
each, all $288\times288$ pixels. Ridges are grown by Gabor-filtering noise along a core--delta
orientation field, as in SFinGe. Every image gets random contrast, blur, noise and dry or wet blotches.
Each material adds one artefact: thick ridges, blur, grey-level quantisation, moisture, ridge breaks, or
elastic distortion. The artefact has a random strength and covers 40--100\% of the print, so where a
patch lands matters. These are loose analogies, not models of silicone or gelatin, so \emph{no absolute
number here says anything about real spoof detection}. The stand-in tests the mechanism: does scoring
$k$ centroid patches instead of every minutia patch lose accuracy, and how does cost scale?

\textbf{Reimplementation.} The authors' code is not public, so the pipeline was written from scratch for
this project (see the AI-use statement). Library code is used only for the MobileNet-v1 architecture (Keras),
k-means (scikit-learn) and basic image operations. \texttt{bash run\_all.sh --synthetic} regenerates
every number and figure from a clean checkout, and the same pipeline runs on real data by pointing it at
a folder.

\textbf{Minutiae.} The paper does not name its extractor. The one written here uses local normalisation, a
structure-tensor orientation field, Gabor enhancement, skeletonisation and the crossing number, then
removes points near the print edge and pairs closer than one ridge period. It finds \MinutiaeMean\
minutiae per image, close to the paper's 35, so the all-minutiae baseline costs \CallsAll\ CNN calls.

\textbf{Centroid alignment.} A centroid is not a minutia and has no direction. The paper does not say
how centroid patches were rotated, so each one is aligned to the ridge orientation field at the centroid
(defined only modulo $180^\circ$).

\textbf{Classifier.} The classifier is MobileNet-v1 ($\alpha=1$) with a $96\times96$ input, trained on minutia-centred
patches that inherit their image's label. The final feature map is $3\times3$, so global average pooling
matches the paper's $3\times3$ pool. Keras is used because TF-Slim needs TensorFlow~1.x. The paper gives
no training recipe. Models were trained on \TrainHardware, \TrainInit: Adam with learning rate $10^{\LRexp}$,
batch 128, \NEpochs\ epochs, class-balanced loss, dropout 0.2, and at most \PatchCap\ patches per image.
A first run trained from scratch on a CPU showed validation AUC stuck at exactly 0.5 for several epochs
while training AUC rose: the BatchNorm running statistics lagged the weights, so inference outputs were
constant. As a final training step, those statistics are therefore recomputed with the final weights,
without changing any weights. For the reported seed-0 model, validation patch AUC is \ValAUCBefore\
before this step and \ValAUCAfter\ after it.

\textbf{Protocol.} One trained model scores every setting, as in the paper. For each
$k\in\{5,10,15,20\}$, k-means is repeated with 5 seeds ($n_\text{init}=1$). The threshold is set per
setting so that at most 0.2\% of bona fide test images score above it. TDR at FDR~=~1\% and a bootstrap s.d.\ are also
reported. For timing, the model runs as 8-bit TFLite on one CPU thread, one patch at a time
like the phone app, on 100 test images. Only the scaling with $k$ is comparable to the paper.

Because the data differ, the like-for-like quantity is the \emph{change} from the all-minutiae
baseline (Table~\ref{tab:main}, Fig.~\ref{fig:tdr}). The reproduced baseline is \TDRall\% (bootstrap s.d.\
\TDRallBootSD). With 10 centroid patches it is \TDRkTen~$\pm$~\TDRkTenSD\%, a change of \DeltaKTen\
points against the paper's $-0.4$.
This part of the claim does \emph{not} reproduce. TDR keeps rising with $k$: \DeltaKFive\ points at
$k=5$, \DeltaKTen\ at $k=10$, and still about four points below the baseline at $k=15$--20
(within half a point at FDR~=~1\%). In the paper the curve is
already flat at $k=10$. At FDR~=~1\%, where the threshold is less noisy, the change at $k=10$ is smaller
(\DeltaOneKTen\ points) but still beyond the one-point criterion.

The absolute level is close to the paper's (\TDRall\% against 95.7\%), but this is a coincidence of
the synthetic data, not a reproduction: the materials are artificial, and one of them (``printed'',
Table~\ref{tab:mat}) remains hard. What matters is that the detector is now strong enough for a loss to
show. An earlier CPU run, trained from scratch with 12 patches per image, reached only about 35\% TDR,
and at that level the $k=10$ loss was hidden in the noise.

\section{Numbers the paper does not report}

``Centroid'': the paper's method. ``Unweighted'': plain mean of the centroid scores. ``Nearest minutia'':

\label{tab:abl}\resizebox{\columnwidth}{!}{\input{../results/table_ablation.tex}}
\end{table}

\textbf{Clustering, or just fewer patches?} The paper never compares k-means with simply classifying $k$
random minutiae. At $k=10$, random minutiae give \TDRrandTen~$\pm$~\TDRrandTenSD\%, against
\TDRkTen~$\pm$~\TDRkTenSD\% for centroids (Table~\ref{tab:abl}). Random minutiae do \emph{better} than centroids at $k=10$, and match them at $k=15$ and 20. Whatever
helps here is not where the clusters fall.

\textbf{Does the size weighting matter?} Without weights, 10 centroids give
\TDRunwTen~$\pm$~\TDRunwTenSD\%, better than the weighted rule. The paper motivates the weighting but
never tests it. On this data it hurts, possibly because a few large clusters dominate the score.

\textbf{More training seeds.} Models were trained with \NSeeds\ different seeds (Table~\ref{tab:seeds}).
Their all-minutiae TDR ranges from \AllSeedsMin\% to \AllSeedsMax\%, so retraining alone moves the
baseline by more than the paper's whole $k=10$ effect. The change at $k=10$ is
\DeltaSeedsMean~$\pm$~\DeltaSeedsSD\ points across seeds (range \DeltaSeedsMin\ to \DeltaSeedsMax), and
\NSeedsPass\ of the \NSeeds\ seeds meet the one-point criterion. At FDR~=~1\%, where the threshold is less noisy, the change is \DeltaOneSeedsMean~$\pm$~\DeltaOneSeedsSD:
every seed loses accuracy, and by almost the same amount. Seed~0's much larger loss at FDR~=~0.2\% appears to
come mostly from its threshold, set by one unusual bona fide image (bootstrap s.d.\ \TDRallBootSD\ points).
With a strong detector, the answer to the research question is therefore \emph{no}: 10 centroid patches
lose more than one point.

instead of recomputed, as stated in the proposal.}
\label{tab:seeds}\resizebox{\columnwidth}{!}{\input{../results/table_seeds.tex}}

\textbf{One threshold for every setting.} The paper recomputes the threshold for each $k$, but a deployed
system would keep one. Reusing the all-minutiae threshold at $k=10$ gives TDR~=~\FixedTDRkTen\% at an
actual FDR of \FixedFDRkTen\%, compared with the 0.2\% target. Centroid patches shift the bona fide
scores up, so a device switching to $k=10$ would have to recalibrate its threshold. The paper's
per-setting thresholds hide this.

\section{What did not reproduce}
\subsection{The data, and therefore the absolute numbers}
The largest gap could not be closed. Without MSU-FPAD and PBSKD it is impossible to test whether 95.3\% and
95.7\% hold, or whether the result holds on real materials such as silicone, gelatin and Play-Doh, which
fool CNNs in ways these artefacts do not. The synthetic results test only whether centroid patches are
enough for a patch classifier \emph{in principle}. Everything in this section that does not depend on
the data, namely the latency analysis and the paper's internal consistency, could be checked fully.

\subsection{The speed-up}

proportional to patch count. The paper's points fall below it. The reproduction's CNN stage follows it, and its total
lies above it because minutiae extraction is a fixed cost.}\label{fig:lat}
\end{figure}
\begin{table}[t]
\centering\caption{Per-image latency (ms) by stage: one CPU thread, TFLite with 8-bit weights, 100 test
images. Minutiae extraction is the same for every $k$.}
\label{tab:timing}\resizebox{\columnwidth}{!}{\input{../results/table_timing.tex}}
\end{table}
Cutting from \CallsAll\ patches to 10 makes the CNN stage \SpeedupCNN$\times$ faster, roughly in
proportion to the patch count. The whole pipeline, however, goes from \MsAll~ms to \MsKTen~ms, only
\SpeedupTotal$\times$ faster (Table~\ref{tab:timing}). Minutiae extraction takes \MsMinutiae~ms per image
whatever $k$ is: clustering needs the minutiae first, so it can only shrink the CNN stage. The paper does
not say whether its 302~ms includes minutiae extraction. If it does, the phone must extract minutiae far
faster, relative to one CNN call, than the extractor written here does.

\subsection{Table~2 is not internally consistent}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Latency per patch.} For $k=5$ to 30, Table~2 implies about 30~ms per patch (148/5, 302/10,
910/30), which matches the 30~ms per quantised patch given in Section~3. At that rate all 35 minutiae
would take about 1,050~ms, not 1,554~ms, so the baseline row carries about 500~ms that the clustered rows
do not. Fig.~\ref{fig:lat} shows this: the paper's points fall \emph{below} the proportional line, which
cannot happen if cost scales only with the number of patches. The claimed 5.1$\times$ speed-up
(1,554/302) exceeds the 3.5$\times$ cut in patches (35/10). The paper itself says ``approximately
4-fold'' in one place and ``almost 80\%'' in another.
\item \textbf{The baseline TDR.} Section~2.4 gives TDR~=~97.20\% at FDR~=~0.2\% with all materials
known in training. Table~2's all-minutiae row, also with all materials known, is 95.7\%. The paper does
not explain the gap: the $96\times96$ retraining, quantisation or a different split could each cause it.
\item \textbf{What the s.d.\ measures.} The all-minutiae row has s.d.\ 0.1 although it involves no k-means
at all. Its variation must come from something other than initialisation, which suggests the ``5 folds''
are five data splits. If so, the $\pm0.5$ at $k=10$ mixes split noise with initialisation noise, and the
s.d.\ over k-means seeds on one split is not the same quantity.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The operating point is fragile}
At FDR~=~0.2\% with 1,000 bona fide images, the threshold is the third-highest bona fide score, so one or
two unusual live prints decide the result. With \NTestBona\ bona fide test images it is the
second-highest score, and the bootstrap s.d.\ for the baseline is \TDRallBootSD\ points (seed~0) and
\TDRallBootSDSeedOne\ (seed~1). Seed~0's error is large because one bona fide image sets its threshold.
But even the binomial error of a TDR near 95\%, measured on about 1,000 spoofs, is roughly 0.7 points:
larger than the paper's 0.4-point gap, and far larger than its $\pm0.1$. The paper does not state its
spoof test count, so this is an estimate. Either way, Table~2 alone cannot show that $k=10$ and all
minutiae perform the same.

\subsection{What was tried}
The model is trained on minutia-centred patches but tested on centroid-centred ones, and a centroid
often lies between minutiae. To test whether this mismatch costs accuracy, each patch was moved to the
minutia nearest its centroid, keeping clusters and weights. This gave \TDRnearTen~$\pm$~\TDRnearTenSD\%
at $k=10$ (Table~\ref{tab:abl}), against \TDRkTen\% for centroids: snapping recovers about five of
the ten points lost. The mismatch between minutia-centred training patches and centroid-centred test
patches is therefore a real cause of the loss, and one the paper does not discuss. A model trained on
centroid patches might remove it; there was no time to test that. Two other problems had to be fixed
along the way: the BatchNorm recalibration described in Section~II, and a first CPU run of one seed that
was killed for lack of memory and had to be rerun alone.

\subsection{What remains unexplained}
First, how the paper's model avoided the centroid-patch loss. It may have aligned or trained on
centroid patches differently; Section~3 of the paper gives no detail. Second, the roughly 500~ms of the paper's baseline latency that per-patch cost does not
account for. Third, the paper's own 97.2\% versus 95.7\%. And fourth, the question synthetic data cannot
reach: whether the flat curve holds for real materials, where a spoof can look bona fide in one region and
fake in another.

\section{Data ethics}
The paper's datasets contain fingerprints of real people, captured under MSU's consent and released only
to researchers who sign a data agreement. That gating is why they could not be obtained in time, and it is
also why I did not use any unofficial copy: doing so would breach the terms under which the subjects'
data was shared. All results here use synthetic fingerprints, so the repository contains no one's
biometric data. When run on real data, the code keeps images, patches and minutiae out of version
control, because minutiae coordinates are a biometric template. Presentation-attack research is also
dual-use: per-material results can show an attacker which materials evade a detector.

\section{Conclusion}
The paper's data were out of reach, so the mechanism could be tested but not the numbers. On synthetic
fingerprints, with a detector at about 90\% TDR, the central claim does not reproduce: 10 centroid
patches lose \DeltaSeedsMean\ points on average across \NSeeds\ seeds at FDR~=~0.2\%, and
\DeltaOneSeedsMean\ at FDR~=~1\%, against the paper's 0.4. Most of that loss comes from cutting patches
at centroids rather than minutiae. The fourfold CNN saving reproduces, but the end-to-end speed-up is
\SpeedupTotal$\times$, not five, because minutiae extraction cannot be clustered away. The paper's own
latencies also imply a baseline about 500~ms heavier than its per-patch cost explains. The most
defensible number is the stage breakdown in Table~\ref{tab:timing}, because it does not depend on the
unobtainable data.
