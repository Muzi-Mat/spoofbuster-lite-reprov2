\title{Reproducing Minutiae Clustering in Fingerprint Spoof Buster Lite, Without the Paper's Data}
\author{Muzi \todo{surname} (\todo{student number}), University of the Witwatersrand\\
Repository: \url{https://github.com/TODO/spoofbuster-lite-repro}}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Chugh and Jain~\cite{chugh2019} report that classifying patches at 10 k-means centroids of the minutiae,
instead of at every minutia, keeps fingerprint presentation-attack detection at TDR~=~95.3\% (against
95.7\%) at FDR~=~0.2\%, while cutting phone inference time from 1,554~ms to 302~ms. The paper's data is
gated by a Michigan State University agreement, and I could not obtain it, so I reimplemented the full
pipeline and tested the claim's mechanism on seeded synthetic fingerprints. With 10 centroid patches, TDR
changed by \DeltaKTen\ points from the all-minutiae baseline, within my one-point criterion. A second
training seed, however, gave \DeltaKTenSeedOne, and the baseline's sampling error is \TDRallBootSD\
points, so the effect is below what I can resolve. Clustering cut CNN time \SpeedupCNN$\times$ but total
latency only \SpeedupTotal$\times$, because minutiae extraction dominates. I also show that the paper's
Table~2 is internally inconsistent.
\end{abstract}

\section{The claim}
Fingerprint Spoof Buster~\cite{chugh2018} detects presentation attacks (spoofs) by cutting a
$96\times96$ patch around every minutia, aligned to the minutia direction, scoring each patch with
MobileNet-v1~\cite{howard2017} and averaging the scores. Its cost grows with the number of minutiae, 35
per image in the paper's data. Section~3 of~\cite{chugh2019} proposes Spoof Buster Lite: cluster the
minutia coordinates with k-means, cut one patch at each centroid, and average the patch scores weighted
by cluster size.

The claim I set out to reproduce is in Table~2 of~\cite{chugh2019}: \emph{with $k=10$ clusters, TDR at
FDR~=~0.2\% falls only from 95.7\% to 95.3\%, while inference time falls from 1,554~ms to 302~ms.} TDR is
the fraction of spoofs detected, and FDR the fraction of bona fide prints wrongly flagged. The support
is that one table: six values of $k$ and the baseline, each given as mean $\pm$ s.d.\ over ``5-fold
cross-validation'', with times from a quantised model on a Redmi Note~4. The data are 5,743 bona fide
and 4,912 spoof images of 12 materials (MSU-FPAD and PBSKD).

My research question, fixed in the proposal: \emph{does $k=10$ keep TDR within one percentage point of
the all-minutiae baseline while reducing the number of MobileNet evaluations?}

\textbf{Synthetic stand-in.} So that the method could still be tested end to end, I generated a seeded
dataset of 1,600 bona fide prints (\NTestBona\ for testing) and 6 artefact ``materials'' of 200 prints
each, all $288\times288$ pixels. Ridges are grown by Gabor-filtering noise along a core--delta
orientation field, as in SFinGe. Every image gets random contrast, blur, noise and dry or wet blotches.
Each material adds one artefact: thick ridges, blur, grey-level quantisation, moisture, ridge breaks, or
elastic distortion. The artefact has a random strength and covers 40--100\% of the print, so where a
patch lands matters. These are loose analogies, not models of silicone or gelatin, so \emph{no absolute
number here says anything about real spoof detection}. The stand-in tests the mechanism: does scoring
$k$ centroid patches instead of every minutia patch lose accuracy, and how does cost scale?

\textbf{Reimplementation.} The authors' code is not public, so all code is mine, written with AI
assistance and checked line by line. I used library code only for the MobileNet-v1 architecture (Keras),
k-means (scikit-learn) and basic image operations. \texttt{bash run\_all.sh --synthetic} regenerates
every number and figure from a clean checkout, and the same pipeline runs on real data by pointing it at
a folder.

\textbf{Minutiae.} The paper does not name its extractor. Mine uses local normalisation, a
structure-tensor orientation field, Gabor enhancement, skeletonisation and the crossing number, then
removes points near the print edge and pairs closer than one ridge period. It finds \MinutiaeMean\
minutiae per image, close to the paper's 35, so the all-minutiae baseline costs \CallsAll\ CNN calls.

\textbf{Centroid alignment.} A centroid is not a minutia and has no direction. The paper does not say
how centroid patches were rotated, so I align each one to the ridge orientation field at the centroid
(defined only modulo $180^\circ$).

\textbf{Classifier.} I use MobileNet-v1 ($\alpha=1$) with a $96\times96$ input, trained on minutia-centred
patches that inherit their image's label. The final feature map is $3\times3$, so global average pooling
matches the paper's $3\times3$ pool. I used Keras because TF-Slim needs TensorFlow~1.x. The paper gives
no training recipe. On a 2-core CPU without ImageNet weights, I trained from scratch: Adam with learning
rate $10^{-3}$, batch 128, 5 epochs, class-balanced loss, dropout 0.2, and at most 12 patches per image.
For the first epochs, validation AUC sat at exactly 0.5 while training AUC rose: the BatchNorm running
statistics lagged the weights, so inference outputs were constant. As a final training step I recompute
those statistics with the final weights, without changing any weights. This raised seed-0 validation
patch AUC from 0.813 to 0.842.

\textbf{Protocol.} One trained model scores every setting, as in the paper. For each
$k\in\{5,10,15,20\}$, k-means is repeated with 5 seeds ($n_\text{init}=1$). The threshold is set per
setting so that at most 0.2\% of bona fide test images score above it. I also report TDR at FDR~=~1\%
and a bootstrap s.d. For timing, the model runs as 8-bit TFLite on one CPU thread, one patch at a time
like the phone app, on 100 test images. Only the scaling with $k$ is comparable to the paper.

The shape matches the paper. Both curves dip at $k=5$ (mine \DeltaKFive, the paper's $-1.8$) and are
flat from $k=10$ upwards, so on this point the claim reproduces: about 10 centroid patches carry as much
information for the classifier as all \CallsAll\ minutia patches. At FDR~=~1\%, where the threshold is
less noisy, the change at $k=10$ is \DeltaOneKTen\ points.

is weak. It was trained from scratch for 5 epochs on 12 patches per image, and two of my synthetic
materials are almost undetectable (Table~\ref{tab:mat}). ``No loss'' at 35\% TDR is weaker evidence than
``no loss'' at 95\%, because a detector near its ceiling has less room to lose.

\textbf{Clustering, or just fewer patches?} The paper never compares k-means with simply classifying $k$
random minutiae. At $k=10$, random minutiae give \TDRrandTen~$\pm$~\TDRrandTenSD\%, against
\TDRkTen~$\pm$~\TDRkTenSD\% for centroids (Table~\ref{tab:abl}). The means are within noise of each
other, but random selection varies about three times as much between seeds at $k=10$. Here, clustering
seems to buy stability rather than accuracy: centroids spread the patches over the whole print, while a
random draw can leave out the region that carries the artefact. At $k=15$ and 20, the two methods
coincide.

\textbf{Does the size weighting matter?} Without weights, 10 centroids give
\TDRunwTen~$\pm$~\TDRunwTenSD\%, no worse than the weighted rule. The paper motivates the weighting but
never tests it. On my data it does not help.

\textbf{A second training seed.} A model retrained with a different seed reaches \TDRallSeedOne\% with
all minutiae (Table~\ref{tab:seeds}), against \TDRall\% for seed~0: retraining alone moves the
baseline by more than the paper's whole $k=10$ effect. For seed~1, $k=10$ scores
\TDRkTenSeedOne~$\pm$~\TDRkTenSDSeedOne\%, a change of \DeltaKTenSeedOne\ points, which \emph{fails} the
one-point criterion. At FDR~=~1\%, the seed-1 change is \DeltaOneKTenSeedOne. My answer to the research
question therefore depends on the training seed. The honest conclusion is that the effect of clustering
is smaller than the noise my setup can resolve, not that it is zero.

\textbf{One threshold for every setting.} The paper recomputes the threshold for each $k$, but a deployed
system would keep one. Reusing the all-minutiae threshold at $k=10$ gives TDR~=~\FixedTDRkTen\% at an
actual FDR of \FixedFDRkTen\%, compared with the 0.2\% target. Centroid patches shift the bona fide
scores up slightly, so a device switching to $k=10$ would have to recalibrate its threshold. The paper's
per-setting thresholds hide this.

\section{What did not reproduce}
\subsection{The data, and therefore the absolute numbers}
The largest gap is the one I could not close. Without MSU-FPAD and PBSKD I cannot test whether 95.3\% and
95.7\% hold, or whether the result holds on real materials such as silicone, gelatin and Play-Doh, which
fool CNNs in ways my artefacts do not. My synthetic results test only whether centroid patches are
enough for a patch classifier \emph{in principle}. Everything in this section that does not depend on
the data, namely the latency analysis and the paper's internal consistency, I could check fully.

Cutting from \CallsAll\ patches to 10 makes the CNN stage \SpeedupCNN$\times$ faster, roughly in
proportion to the patch count. The whole pipeline, however, goes from \MsAll~ms to \MsKTen~ms, only
\SpeedupTotal$\times$ faster (Table~\ref{tab:timing}). Minutiae extraction takes \MsMinutiae~ms per image
whatever $k$ is: clustering needs the minutiae first, so it can only shrink the CNN stage. The paper does
not say whether its 302~ms includes minutiae extraction. If it does, the phone must extract minutiae far
faster, relative to one CNN call, than my extractor does.

\subsection{Table~2 is not internally consistent}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Latency per patch.} For $k=5$ to 30, Table~2 implies about 30~ms per patch (148/5, 302/10,
910/30), which matches the 30~ms per quantised patch given in Section~3. At that rate all 35 minutiae
would take about 1,050~ms, not 1,554~ms, so the baseline row carries about 500~ms that the clustered rows
do not. Fig.~\ref{fig:lat} shows this: the paper's points fall \emph{below} the proportional line, which
cannot happen if cost scales only with the number of patches. The claimed 5.1$\times$ speed-up
(1,554/302) exceeds the 3.5$\times$ cut in patches (35/10). The paper itself says ``approximately
4-fold'' in one place and ``almost 80\%'' in another.
\item \textbf{The baseline TDR.} Section~2.4 gives TDR~=~97.20\% at FDR~=~0.2\% with all materials
known in training. Table~2's all-minutiae row, also with all materials known, is 95.7\%. The paper does
not explain the gap: the $96\times96$ retraining, quantisation or a different split could each cause it.
\item \textbf{What the s.d.\ measures.} The all-minutiae row has s.d.\ 0.1 although it involves no k-means
at all. Its variation must come from something other than initialisation, which suggests the ``5 folds''
are five data splits. If so, the $\pm0.5$ at $k=10$ mixes split noise with initialisation noise, and my
s.d.\ over k-means seeds on one split is not the same quantity.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The operating point is fragile}
At FDR~=~0.2\% with 1,000 bona fide images, the threshold is the third-highest bona fide score, so one or
two unusual live prints decide the result. With my \NTestBona\ bona fide test images it is the
second-highest score, and my bootstrap s.d.\ for the baseline is \TDRallBootSD\ points (seed~0) and
\TDRallBootSDSeedOne\ (seed~1). My detector is weaker, so its error is larger than the paper's would be.
But even the binomial error of a TDR near 95\%, measured on about 1,000 spoofs, is roughly 0.7 points:
larger than the paper's 0.4-point gap, and far larger than its $\pm0.1$. The paper does not state its
spoof test count, so this is an estimate. Either way, Table~2 alone cannot show that $k=10$ and all
minutiae perform the same.

\subsection{What I tried}
The model is trained on minutia-centred patches but tested on centroid-centred ones, and a centroid
often lies between minutiae. To test whether this mismatch costs accuracy, I moved each patch to the
minutia nearest its centroid, keeping clusters and weights. This gave \TDRnearTen~$\pm$~\TDRnearTenSD\%
at $k=10$ (Table~\ref{tab:abl}), the same as centroids within noise. The centroid--minutia mismatch is
therefore not what limits accuracy here. Two other problems had to be fixed along the way. The
BatchNorm recalibration described in Section~II was needed before the from-scratch models gave
non-constant outputs. And the first run of the second seed was killed for lack of memory when I ran a
test alongside it on the same 8~GB machine, so I reran it alone.

\subsection{What I still cannot explain}
First, why the second seed shows a three-point drop at $k=10$ when the first shows none. With two seeds
I cannot tell whether this is a real seed effect or threshold noise; it would take many more seeds than my
CPU budget allowed. Second, the roughly 500~ms of the paper's baseline latency that per-patch cost does not
account for. Third, the paper's own 97.2\% versus 95.7\%. And fourth, the question my synthetic data cannot
reach: whether the flat curve holds for real materials, where a spoof can look bona fide in one region and
fake in another.

\section{Data ethics}
The paper's datasets contain fingerprints of real people, captured under MSU's consent and released only
to researchers who sign a data agreement. That gating is why I could not obtain them in time, and it is
also why I did not use any unofficial copy: doing so would breach the terms under which the subjects'
data was shared. All results here use synthetic fingerprints, so the repository contains no one's
biometric data. When run on real data, the code keeps images, patches and minutiae out of version
control, because minutiae coordinates are a biometric template. Presentation-attack research is also
dual-use: per-material results can show an attacker which materials evade a detector.

\section{Conclusion}
The paper's data were out of reach, so I could reproduce the mechanism but not the numbers. On synthetic
fingerprints, the shape of Table~2 reproduces: TDR dips at $k=5$ and is flat from $k=10$. The size of the
effect is below what my setup can resolve: retraining and the 0.2\% threshold each move TDR by more than
the paper's 0.4 points. The fourfold CNN saving reproduces. The fivefold end-to-end speed-up does not:
minutiae extraction, which clustering cannot remove, keeps the real gain at \SpeedupTotal$\times$, and the
paper's own latencies imply a baseline about 500~ms heavier than its per-patch cost explains. The number I
would defend first is the stage breakdown in Table~\ref{tab:timing}, because it does not depend on the
data I could not obtain.
